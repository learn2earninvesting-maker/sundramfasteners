The Irreplaceability Moat
Why customers can't easily swap Sundram out once a part is designed in.

"Cast in stone" — designed-in parts resist cheaper rivals

Once Sundram wins a programme for a critical component (a part the vehicle cannot function safely without), the carmaker will not switch supplier even if someone quotes a lower price. Management describes this as virtually no competition.

"if it is a critical component, the OEMs are not comfortable replacing. In that sense, virtually there's no competition. For instance, the $250 million program which we have won, if some other supplier, whether in India or some other part of the world, offers a better price, the OEM will not change the source... it is cast in stone from that perspective."Dilip Kumar · Q4 FY23 earnings call

The point: what keeps the business is design-in status — being written into the customer's engineering drawings — not being the cheapest bidder.

The two-tier reality

  • Protected Specials / critical parts: Sundram is the single source with most carmakers. Switching essentially doesn't happen.
  • Contested Standard / entry-level parts: here customers deliberately keep a second supplier to reduce risk, and rivals bid lower. Sundram says it "does not give in" on price.
  • On standard products it is present with 30–40% of each customer's suppliers, but holds 50–60% of that customer's business.
  • Six decades in fasteners, plus joint development with customers across petrol/diesel (ICE), electric (EV) and hybrid programmes, is the underlying barrier.
  • The fasteners business with Cummins is described as growing very well.

Scale and validation

  • India's largest fastener maker — 35–40% market share; management cites close to 40–45%.
  • World-class plants in three countries, headquartered in Chennai.
  • A General Motors supplier for 25+ years, named Supplier of the Year five years running (1996–2000) and again in 2009, 2019, 2020 and 2021 — across Cadillac, GMC and Chevrolet.
  • Exports are roughly 30% of revenue: North America ~65% of that, Europe/UK 20–25%.
Value Chain
What Sundram actually makes, and how far up the chain it has climbed.

Not a fastener company any more

The company describes itself as "a broad-based engineering company capable of undertaking the manufacture of various components and assembly systems for automotive and other engineering applications" — i.e. it sells finished sub-assemblies, not just parts.

The product ladder

  • High-tensile fasteners · powder metal components · cold extruded parts
  • Hot forged components · radiator caps · automotive pumps
  • Cast and machined assemblies · gears and couplings · tappets
  • Iron powder · powertrain components and sub-assemblies

Process depth: forging, metal forming, close-tolerance machining (cutting to very fine accuracy), heat treatment, surface finishing and assembly — all in-house.

The forging factory already exports to large US EV manufacturers, with new lines for precision-machined connecting rods and wheel bearing parts.
The Financial Engine
The 1:1 rule on new investment, and what the reported numbers actually show.

The 1:1 capex-to-revenue rule

The planning assumption: every ₹1 spent on revenue-adding new machinery should generate about ₹1 of extra sales every year.

"We are incurring about Rs. 300 crores. About Rs. 100 crores would be for replacement. A balance Rs. 200 crores would be for revenue. And like I said, a 1:1 is a safe bet to assume."R. Dilip Kumar, CFO · Q3 FY26 earnings call
  • The split: roughly 25–30% of spending is replacement (improves productivity, adds no sales); the remaining 60–65% adds revenue directly.
  • The timing: imported machines take 6–9 months to install; the one-to-one growth then shows up over the following ~6 months.
  • Customer-backed: investment runs on clear indications and agreements from customers — not speculative capacity.
  • Actual FY26 spend: ₹404.27 crore, above the ~₹350 crore guided, for capacity expansion in auto and non-auto.
  • Best case seen: one order needed ₹80 crore of spending for peak revenue of ₹150–160 crore — far better than 1:1.

Why non-auto is supposed to lift returns

  • Wind, aerospace and industrial fasteners are named as the key non-auto drivers because their returns beat auto. Defence stays small — validation and approval take too long.
  • EV economics: guided at ~20–21% operating profit margin versus the company's ~15–16% at the time, with return on capital "closer to 35%", later refined to ~25%.
  • Non-auto is about one-third of revenue (tractors 10–12%), with a stated aim of 50% over the medium to long term.
The caveat that matters: this uplift has not appeared in reported numbers yet. Return on capital has slipped from 18.7% (FY23) to 16.9% (FY26) while fixed assets grew from ₹2,029 cr to ₹2,501 cr.

Transition Dynamics
The electric-vehicle order book, what it costs the company, and the hedge.

The ₹4,000 crore EV order book

  • Anchor A $250 million contract announced January 2023 — the biggest EV contract in the company's 60-year history — for shaft and drive gear sub-assemblies for a leading global carmaker.
  • Structure: ₹200 crore investment, a six-year purchase package, peak sales ~$52 million in 2026, ~0.15 crore drive-unit sub-assemblies a year.
  • Mix: the Feb-2023 read was ~70% pure EV, ~20% hybrids, across SUVs and sedans.
  • Scale: cumulative EV order book ~₹4,000 crore; a $375 million order book as of May 2023.
  • Margin promise: ~20–21% operating margin, return on capital toward 25–35%.

The downside: less content per car, and delays

Content loss: a pure battery-electric vehicle cuts Sundram's steel forging content by 25–30% versus a petrol/diesel car — the engine is replaced by a battery pack, and the engine is where the forgings were.
  • EV pickup has moved out to H2 FY27.
  • Stellantis EV launches have slipped by another quarter.
  • The industry "goalpost" has shifted from 2030 to 2035.

The hedge: an engine-heavy, diversified book

  • Calibrated shift: management expects the mix to settle at roughly 50–60% petrol/diesel, ~15% plug-in hybrid and 25–30% battery-electric by 2030–2035. Engines stay dominant for years.
  • Wind: ~₹200 cr (FY23) → ~₹350 cr annualised by Q3 FY26 → heading to ~₹500 cr (~30% growth) at full use of installed resources.
  • Aerospace: ~₹50 cr today, targeting ₹100 cr then ₹500 cr in 2–3 years; monthly revenue up toward ~₹5 cr from ~₹2.5–3 cr. Customers include General Electric, Hindustan Aeronautics and Skyroads, with NADCAP and AS9100 certifications.
  • Exports stay engine-heavy: cars and commercial vehicles ~80% of exports, tractors ~5%, rest off-road and stationary engines. GM alone contributes upwards of ₹250 cr.
  • Pipeline: over ₹1,000 cr of new projects with a similar amount under discussion; target of at least 20% of revenue from new products on a rolling 3-year basis.
Management & Capital Allocation
TVS Group heritage, the family reorganisation, and the spending track record.

TVS heritage and the promoter reclassification

  • Sundram sits within the TVS Group, whose businesses have traditionally been managed by members of the different branches of the TVS family — mainly third- and fourth-generation descendants of founder T.V. Sundaram Iyengar.
  • Three TVS holding companies held 49.53% of Sundram Fasteners (10,40,85,280 shares).
  • A December 2020 Memorandum of Family Arrangement sought to align ownership with management; a composite scheme would move the fasteners undertaking to TVS Sundram Fasteners Pvt Ltd, with the 49.53% stake vesting there, majority-held by the Sri Suresh Krishna family.
  • Distance from the listed entity: Sundram Fasteners is not a party to the arrangement and said the transaction is not a related-party transaction from its perspective. It signed on to the family's Non-Competition Agreement via a deed of adherence in January 2022.

Spending track record

  • FY24 capex of ₹300 cr spent in line with plan.
  • FY26 actual ₹404.27 cr beat the ~₹350 cr guide.
  • FY27 guided to ~₹250 cr.
  • Directed spending: ₹200 cr to the EV contract, ~₹85–90 cr on wind, ~₹25 cr on Tamil Nadu group-captive power.
The watch-item: operating margin has been strikingly stable (~15–16% across FY23–FY26), but revenue growth slowed to 5.6% in FY26 and return on capital has drifted down. The ~₹1,000 cr spending programme now needs its 1:1 conversion — and the wind/aerospace/EV book — to lift returns. That hasn't happened yet.

What it means in plain English

The irreplaceability story is already largely priced in. The shares trade at roughly their own three-year average earnings multiple, but at a 45% premium to the industry's 29x, with a thin free-cash-flow yield of 1.8% because of the heavy spending cycle.

The single question that decides the outcome: does the 1:1 capex-to-revenue conversion — plus the wind, aerospace and EV book — actually deliver the promised return uplift? Or do programme delays and the 25–30% content loss on battery vehicles keep reported return on capital capped near 16.9%?
